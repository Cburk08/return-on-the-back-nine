Did the new course pay off?

What 33 member-owned clubs’ tax filings show about renovating a golf course: when it paid off, how clubs paid for it, and whether a famous architect was worth the money.

By Colton Burkhalter5-minute readFull analysis: the deck →

Over the last decade, hundreds of private clubs spent millions rebuilding their golf courses. Boards sell these projects to members as an investment: a better course brings in new members, keeps the ones you have, and lifts what people spend at the club. Clubs rarely check afterward whether that happened, and when they do, the numbers stay inside the club.

There is a public record that can answer the question. Most member-owned clubs are tax-exempt social clubs, and every year they file a Form 990 with the IRS. The filing shows revenue, assets and debt, and on a separate line, initiation fees and capital contributions. Strip those out, along with income from outside guests, and what is left is the club’s everyday business: dues, golf, dining and everything else members spend in a normal year. I call it core member revenue.

I pulled those filings for 33 member-owned clubs that reopened a renovated course between fiscal 2015 and 2022, and for 21 similar clubs that did not renovate. Each club’s reopening date and architect comes from published coverage of the project. Then I asked one question: did revenue grow faster at the renovated club than at its peers over the same years?

It depended on when the course reopened

Clubs that reopened before 2020 grew core member revenue a median of +20 points faster than their peers, and 13 of 20 beat them. Clubs that reopened in 2020 to 2022 came in about even, at −2 points.

The renovated clubs did not suddenly do worse in the boom. They grew 37% in the earlier period and 47% in the later one. What changed was everyone else.

Before 2020, a new course was a way to take members from other clubs. In the boom, demand was high everywhere, and that edge disappeared.

The gap opened after the course came back

A fair worry is that clubs that renovate were already on the way up. So I followed each club year by year, from four years before reopening to three years after, next to its own peers.

Before reopening, renovated clubs moved with their peers. Clubs that reopened before 2020 then pulled away once the course came back: +6 points ahead in the first year and +21 by the third. Clubs that reopened during the boom slipped about 6 points behind in the construction year and never caught up. Flat before and a gap after is the pattern you would expect if the new course itself made the difference.

Did a famous architect pay off?

The best-known names of this era, Gil Hanse, Andrew Green, Coore & Crenshaw and Tom Doak, led 9 of the projects. They cost about 1.6 times as much at the median: about $7.4 million against $4.6 million.

On revenue, the premium was not steady. Big-name projects that reopened before 2020 beat other clubs from the same years (+30 points against +14), but there were only 4 of them; those that reopened in the boom trailed (−7 against +5). A famous name’s payoff may show up in other places: championships, reputation and what a membership is worth. It did not show up reliably in the dues line.

Borrow, assess or pay cash?

Boards spend a lot of time on how to pay for a project. In these filings, the choice did not predict the result. Clubs that mostly borrowed did worse than other clubs before 2020 and better in the boom, on small groups. That makes funding a question of fairness and risk, not return. An assessment puts the bill on today’s members, some of whom may leave. A loan spreads it to future members and brings in a lender. Which is better depends on how long members stay and whether the club has a waitlist.

For buyers and investors

Firms like Concert Golf, Arcis and Invited are buying clubs, and a renovated course is part of what they pay for. In an illustrative all-cash deal, revenue has to end about 18% above the peer path for a renovation to keep up with the rest of the deal, and about 21% with leverage. A buyer of several clubs earns about 6 more points a year by picking clubs with room to grow over full ones.

How sure can we be?

About the direction, fairly sure: the timing pattern held in all 10 ways I re-ran the study. About the size, not very. A test that shuffles the results at random finds a gap this large about 12% of the time, so luck cannot be ruled out. Tax filings show revenue, not member happiness. Four of the clubs hosted professional tournaments after reopening, and some ran clubhouse projects at the same time. And a lower number does not mean a renovation failed; it means revenue grew less than at peer clubs over the same years.

The question for a board

A new course is not a guaranteed revenue engine. Before 2020, it was a way to take members from other clubs. In a market where every club is full, that edge disappears. The question to ask is not only “can we afford it?” but “do we have room to grow, or are we already full?”
