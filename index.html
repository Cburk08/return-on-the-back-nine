The financing and returns of private club renovations

Private equity platforms are buying member-owned golf clubs, and a course renovation is the main value-creation lever they underwrite. This book values a typical club, models the renovation as an all-cash deal, an LBO and a roll-up, and tests the revenue lift against IRS filings from 54 clubs.

02Investment thesisReturn on the Back Nine

Member clubs sell below platform multiples; a renovation adds value only where the club can still grow

Platforms like Concert Golf, Arcis and Invited buy member-owned clubs, pay off their debt and fund capital projects, usually a new course. This book asks what that course is worth to a buyer. It values a typical club from its tax filings, measures how much a renovation lifted revenue at 33 clubs against 21 that did not renovate, and runs the result through an all-cash deal, an LBO and a four-club roll-up.

1 · Entry6.3x

The typical club sells for its debt, its other liabilities and $2.0M to members: about 6.3x EBITDA, against about 8x for platforms. The multiple gap is the safe part of the return.

2 · Value creation+17% vs. −1%

Revenue above the peer path for clubs that reopened before 2020, against 2020-22. Before the boom a new course won members from rivals; in the boom every club filled up.

3 · Returns~18% / ~21%

The lift a renovation needs to keep up with the rest of the deal, all cash and at 3x. The study median is 8.2%, so the typical renovation does not earn its cost; a pre-2020-style club comes close.

Underwriting

In a four-club roll-up at 3x, clubs with room to grow earn about 22% on equity and full clubs about 16%. Picking the club matters more than the course.

Key risks

A small sample (33 clubs) and a wide range on the lift (−4% to +20%). Financing choice and big-name architects showed no steady effect. Margins are modeled because Form 990s do not report EBITDA.

Source: IRS Form 990 via ProPublica and IRS e-file returns; author’s analysis. Illustrative, not investment advice.02 / 19

04ValuationReturn on the Back Nine

A member club changes hands near 6.3x EBITDA; platforms are valued near 8x

What a buyer pays for the typical study club, against what platform multiples say it is worth today and in year seven, with and without the renovation. The price is the club’s debt, the other liabilities a buyer takes on, and $2.0M to members.

Valuation range for the typical study club ($M)

  • The price, $11.4M, is 6.3x year-0 EBITDA of $1.81M. At 7x the same club is worth about $1.3M more on day one: the multiple gap is the first source of return.
  • At the study’s median lift, a $6.1M renovation adds about $4.9M of year-7 value at 8x, less than it costs. At the pre-2020 lift it adds about $10.2M, well above the spend.
So whatThe buy is cheap; the renovation is the judgment call. Underwrite it on whether the club can still win members, not on the course.

07Value creation · EvidenceReturn on the Back Nine

Renovated clubs moved with their peers until the course reopened

Median core member revenue, year C−4 = 100

Renovated clubsTheir peers
  • Before reopening, renovated clubs stayed within 4 points of their peers in both eras. The method needs this: the two groups moved together until the work.
  • Clubs that reopened in FY2015-19 pulled ahead after the course came back: +6 points in the first year and +21 by the third.
  • Clubs that reopened in FY2020-22 slipped about 6 points behind in the construction year and stayed about 5 behind. Both groups grew fast: 53% and 58% over seven years.
So whatThe gap opens after the course reopens, not before. That makes the renovation the likely cause before 2020. In the boom it never opened.
C = fiscal year the course reopened. Index = core member revenue over its level in year C−4, times 100. Peers = the comparison clubs each club is measured against, same years. Medians across clubs.07 / 19

15Capex disciplineReturn on the Back Nine

Spending more, or hiring a big-name architect, did not buy more revenue

The renovation budget is the buyer’s biggest controllable cost. Projects led by the best-known architects of this era spent a median $7.4M against $4.6M for everyone else.

Spend vs. result, 27 clubs with measurable spend

Each dot is a club. Further right = spent more; higher = more extra revenue. If spending paid off, the dots would climb to the top right. They don’t.

Hanse, Green, Coore & Crenshaw, DoakEveryone else
+3%Revenue uplift, big-name architects (median of 9); median spend $7.4M
+9%Revenue uplift, everyone else (median of 24); median spend $4.6M

5 of 9 star projects reopened in the boom, so the fair test is within an era. Before 2020: +24% (4 clubs) against +12% (16 clubs). In 2020-22: −5% (5) against +3% (8). The edge flips with the era, on very few clubs.

So whatFor a buyer, the architect is a cost line, not a return driver. Pay for a name only where tournaments or reputation justify it; at a full, waitlisted club the payoff shows up in membership value, not dues.

How it’s measured. Core member revenue is total revenue minus initiation fees, capital contributions and public-use income (Form 990 Part V, lines 12a and 12b). Peers are the comparison clubs whose assets never jumped more than 15% in one year during the club’s window; peer growth is their median. A club counts only if its initiation-fee line is filled in for all four window years. Uplift = (1 + club growth) / (1 + peer growth) − 1.

Limits and next steps

  • Small, famous sample. 33 renovations, many at championship clubs. The goal was 40; about 80 news searches found 33 that pass every rule. A log of all 85 clubs considered, with reasons, is available from the author.
  • Peers screened mostly by data. The ten new peers were also checked against renovation news, and one original peer, Columbus CC, turned out to have renovated and was moved. The other original peers may have done smaller course work.
  • Fiscal years differ. Each is labeled by the year it ends; lining them up with peers is one of the checks.
  • Tournaments. Four clubs in the main result hosted a pro event after reopening.
  • Spend is a floor. Depreciation is netted out, and some clubs ran clubhouse projects in the same years.
  • Revenue is one payoff. At a full club it shows up in initiation fees, waitlists and retention. That is next.

How this was built

  • Data. Every club figure comes from IRS Form 990 filings: ProPublica’s Nonprofit Explorer, plus the IRS’s own e-file returns for recent years and for the debt lines. Dates and architects come from the sources on slide 19.
  • Rules first. The window, peer screen and fee-line rule were set before the new clubs were added. Clubs that broke a rule were dropped or moved to a check, not adjusted.
  • Fair reading. A negative number means revenue grew less than peers’, not that a renovation failed. Credit figures leave names out.
  • AI use. AI (Claude, Anthropic’s model) was used to pull the IRS filings.
